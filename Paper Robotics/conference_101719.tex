\begin{abstract}
The increasing complexity of supply chain operations has accelerated the need for efficient and adaptive intralogistics solutions. Traditional systems such as Automated Guided Vehicles (AGVs) have served as reliable tools in structured environments, but their dependency on fixed paths and external guidance limits flexibility in dynamic contexts. Autonomous Mobile Robots (AMRs) emerge as a transformative technology, leveraging decentralized navigation, real-time mapping, and advanced sensors like Lidar to revolutionize warehouse automation. This paper provides a comprehensive review of AMR technology, focusing on its components, control strategies, and integration in intralogistics. Key challenges, including task optimization and collaborative systems, are discussed, alongside promising advances in algorithms and dynamic planning. By bridging theoretical models with real-world applications, this study highlights the potential of AMRs to redefine the future of warehouse logistics.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection*{\textbf{1.1 Context and Motivation}}
The increasing complexity of supply chain operations, driven by globalization and the rise of e-commerce, has placed immense pressure on warehouse systems to enhance their operational efficiency. Automated systems have long been pivotal in intralogistics, where Automated Guided Vehicles (AGVs) dominated material handling for decades. AGVs, relying on predefined pathways and centralized controls, delivered significant value in structured environments. However, the rigid nature of these systems has become a bottleneck in today’s dynamic and rapidly evolving operational landscapes, particularly where adaptability and autonomy are paramount.

Autonomous Mobile Robots (AMRs) have emerged as the next evolutionary step, addressing the limitations of AGVs. By leveraging technologies such as Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM), Lidar-based navigation, and artificial intelligence, AMRs transcend the constraints of traditional automation. Their decentralized decision-making and real-time adaptability mark a transformative shift in warehouse automation, empowering businesses to meet modern logistical challenges.

\subsection*{\textbf{1.2 Transition from AGVs to AMRs}}
Since their introduction in the mid-20th century, AGVs have been characterized as reliable, fixed-path material handlers, capable of executing repetitive tasks with minimal human intervention. Their dependency on external infrastructure, such as magnetic tapes or embedded wires, has made them suitable for controlled settings but less adaptable to environments with dynamic layouts or variable tasks.

AMRs, by contrast, represent a paradigm shift. Equipped with advanced sensory and computational capabilities, they navigate autonomously within unstructured environments. Technologies such as SLAM enable AMRs to create detailed maps of their surroundings, dynamically recalibrate their paths, and avoid obstacles without external guidance. This evolution has significantly reduced setup costs, increased operational flexibility, and unlocked new applications across industries, from manufacturing to healthcare and e-commerce.

\subsection*{\textbf{1.3} Objectives and Contributions}
This paper aims to explore the technological evolution and impact of AMRs in modern intralogistics systems. The primary objectives are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item **Evaluate AGV navigation systems vs AMRs**: Comparing their methodologies, limitations, and applications in structured and dynamic environments.
    \item **Explore technological advances**: Investigating how AMRs integrate advanced technologies like SLAM.

    \item **Assess kinematic modeling**: Highlighting the importance of mathematical models in trajectory planning and control for AMR efficiency.
\end{itemize}

\section{Technological Transition from AGVs to AMRs}

\subsection*{\textbf{2.1 Navigation Systems in AGVs}}
Automated Guided Vehicles (AGVs) rely on predefined pathways for navigation. These pathways are often determined through technologies such as wire guidance, magnetic tape, or optical sensors. Wire guidance involves embedding wires into the floor, which emit electrical signals detected by sensors on the AGV. Although effective, this approach is costly, requires substantial infrastructural changes, and lacks flexibility. Optical guidance offers an improvement by using markers or reflective strips, but it still relies heavily on environmental cues, limiting adaptability to dynamic changes.

\subsection*{\textbf{2.2 Limitations of AGV Technology}}
While AGVs are adept at following strict routines in controlled environments, their inability to maneuver around unexpected obstacles significantly restricts their operational range. When faced with obstructions, AGVs typically halt until manual intervention clears the path. This dependency on predefined routes and lack of dynamic decision-making makes AGVs unsuitable for modern, flexible intralogistics systems.

\subsection*{\textbf{2.3 Technological Advancements in AMRs}}
Autonomous Mobile Robots (AMRs) eliminate the constraints of predefined pathways through advanced navigation systems. Utilizing technologies like Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM), Lidar, and 3D cameras, AMRs dynamically map their surroundings, detect obstacles, and calculate optimal routes in real-time. This ability to adapt to environmental changes without human intervention makes them ideal for dynamic and unpredictable operational settings.

\subsection*{\textbf{2.4 Comparative Analysis: AGVs vs AMRs}}
The transition from AGVs to AMRs marks a significant evolution in intralogistics. While AGVs excel in repetitive, structured tasks, AMRs bring unprecedented flexibility and scalability. This flexibility is achieved through decentralized decision-making and advanced sensor integration, enabling AMRs to perform complex tasks like inventory management, collaborative operations with human workers, and navigating high-traffic areas efficiently.

\section{Technological Advances in AMRs}

\subsection*{\textbf{3.1 Advanced Sensors and Navigation Systems}}
To navigate autonomously and perceive their surroundings, Autonomous Mobile Robots (AMRs) depend on advanced sensory systems. LiDAR provides precision mapping and obstacle detection at considerable distances, while 3D cameras add a layer of environmental awareness by offering rich visual data for object identification. Inertial sensors further enhance navigation by supplying motion and orientation details, ensuring stability and adaptability during operations.

The integration of these sensors through sophisticated fusion techniques allows AMRs to construct real-time maps and determine their precise location using Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) \cite{1}. This multi-sensory approach equips AMRs to adapt seamlessly to changing environments, such as dynamic warehouse layouts, without requiring pre-installed navigation aids.

\subsection*{\textbf{3.2 Hardware and Software Integration}}
The evolution of specialized AI processors, such as the NVIDIA Orin series or equivalent advanced processors, has enabled AMRs to process large volumes of data locally, reducing dependence on centralized servers and improving real-time efficiency. 

Advances in lithium-ion batteries have provided longer autonomy and shorter recharge times, which are crucial for continuous operations. Emerging technologies, such as wireless charging, are also being developed to eliminate downtime caused by traditional charging systems.

\subsection*{\textbf{3.3 Decentralized Control and Collaboration}}
Decentralization is a critical enabler of AMR flexibility, particularly in environments requiring real-time adaptability. By distributing decision-making processes across the fleet, AMRs can dynamically assign tasks, adapt routes, and coordinate operations without reliance on a central control unit. This approach enhances system resilience—if one robot encounters an issue, others can compensate without disrupting the entire workflow.

In collaborative environments, decentralized control enables seamless interaction between AMRs, other robots, and human operators. For instance, in a high-traffic warehouse, AMRs can independently negotiate paths to avoid congestion, ensuring efficient task execution. This autonomy also supports scalability, as adding new robots does not significantly increase system complexity or create bottlenecks.

Additionally, advanced algorithms, including reinforcement learning, have enhanced multi-robot collaboration, enabling fleets to optimize operations and avoid collisions in congested settings. Such decentralized strategies align with the demands of modern logistics, where adaptability and efficiency are paramount.

\subsection*{\textbf{3.4 Emerging Technologies}}
Edge computing has gained prominence, enabling local processing of complex tasks such as computer vision for real-time object identification. This transformation is particularly evident in settings where connectivity or latency challenges hinder centralized systems. For example, in warehouses with high data throughput, edge computing allows robots to analyze sensor inputs and make immediate decisions without waiting for remote server responses. This enhances efficiency in time-sensitive operations, such as collision avoidance or dynamic path planning.

The future potential of edge computing lies in its scalability and adaptability. As AMRs become integral to industries beyond logistics—such as healthcare or disaster response—edge computing will support their autonomy in environments where reliable connectivity is unavailable. Furthermore, innovations in edge AI hardware will continue to expand the scope of on-board processing, enabling AMRs to undertake more complex tasks with minimal infrastructure dependencies.

Other innovations include the use of genetic algorithms and swarm intelligence to coordinate large AMR fleets and the development of robotic manipulators that extend their capabilities beyond transport to assembly and item handling.
